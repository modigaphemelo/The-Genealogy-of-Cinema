Queen of Katwe

Mbembe's Chess as Brutalist Improvisation in the Postcolonial Slum
Year 2016
Director Mira Nair
Analysis via Achille Mbembe
Genre Biography / Drama

Mira Nair's Queen of Katwe presents itself as an inspirational sports biography about Phiona Mutesi, a Ugandan chess prodigy from the slums of Kampala. Through Achille Mbembe's lens of necropolitics and what he terms "brutalist improvisation," it reveals itself as a profound meditation on how postcolonial subjects navigate what Mbembe calls the "death worlds" of urban poverty through tactical intelligence. The film's central metaphor—chess as a game of life and death—becomes not just a narrative device but the structural manifestation of what Mbembe describes as the "improvisational strategies" of survival in zones of abandonment.

The Architecture of Slum Ontology

Mbembe, in his work on On the Postcolony and necropolitics, argues that contemporary urban Africa is characterized by what he calls "infrastructural warfare"—the systematic denial of basic resources to certain populations. The Katwe slum in Kampala is precisely such a zone of what Mbembe terms "abandonment." Each flooded alley, each makeshift dwelling, each day without clean water is not merely poverty but a zone of mortality, where life is lived in what Mbembe would call "permanent precariousness." This environmental precarity is the spatialization of necropower: the state's sovereignty is measured by what it fails to provide rather than what it offers.

"In chess, the small one can become the big one."

This line, delivered by Robert Katende (David Oyelowo), the chess coach who mentors Phiona, captures the film's central Mbembian insight. The problem is not Phiona's intelligence—she demonstrates remarkable strategic thinking—but the distribution of opportunity. Chess becomes what Mbembe terms a "tactic of improvisation"—a way to navigate a world organized around her potential death rather than her potential life. This is not mere sportsmanship but something more primal: necropolitical resistance in its purest form, where cognitive power becomes the weapon against physical deprivation.

The Three Necropolitical Subjects of Katwe

The film presents three archetypal responses to what Mbembe calls the "postcolony's brutal materiality," each representing different survival strategies in zones of abandonment:

  1. Phiona Mutesi (Madina Nalwanga): The strategic improviser who transforms chess into what Mbembe calls a "technology of the self." She represents what Mbembe terms the "postcolonial subject as tactical agent"—using the game's abstract logic to create mental escape from physical confinement. Her chess mastery isn't about competition but about what Mbembe describes as "reconfiguring one's relationship to constraint."
  2. Harriet Mutesi (Lupita Nyong'o): The resilient mother who embodies what Mbembe calls "survival as daily warfare." She represents the postcolonial feminine subject who must navigate both state abandonment and patriarchal domination. Her selling of maize isn't mere subsistence but what Mbembe might term "informal economy as necropolitical negotiation."
  3. Robert Katende (David Oyelowo): The mediator who creates what Mbembe calls "zones of exception" within zones of abandonment. His chess program represents not charity but what Mbembe terms "improvisational infrastructure"—creating alternative networks of support where state systems have failed.

Chess as Necropolitical Allegory

The film's most brilliant conceptual move—chess as metaphor for slum survival—functions as what Mbembe would term "allegorical improvisation." In a world organized around premature death, chess represents not intellectual sport but the cognitive mapping of constraint. The chessboard's sixty-four squares become what Mbembe calls "the grid of possibility" in a world of limited options. This mirrors Mbembe's analysis of how postcolonial subjects develop "improvisational epistemologies" to navigate systems designed for their failure.

When Phiona visualizes chess moves while walking through flooded Katwe, she performs what Mbembe calls "tactical cognition"—using abstract thinking to transform physical reality. Her journey from local tournaments to international competitions becomes a necropolitical pilgrimage, moving from zones of abandonment to zones of privilege while carrying the cognitive strategies developed in precarity. Each match represents a different "necropolitical calculation"—a different way of allocating limited resources (pieces) against overwhelming odds.

The Failure of Conventional Uplift Narratives

The film's refusal to become a simple rags-to-riches story—its insistence on showing Phiona's setbacks and returns to Katwe—is profoundly Mbembian. It suggests that individual escape doesn't dismantle systems of abandonment. Phiona's victories don't drain the slum's floods or provide clean water for her family. This reflects Mbembe's skepticism about "neoliberal exceptionalism": the belief that individual success stories can compensate for structural violence. Necropolitical systems, Mbembe argues, tolerate individual escape while maintaining collective abandonment.

The Slum as Chessboard of Constraint

The film's visual contrast between the ordered geometry of chessboards and the chaotic improvisation of Katwe's architecture is essential to its Mbembian critique. Mbembe emphasizes that postcolonial urban spaces operate through "improvised ordering" within apparent disorder. The chessboard's clean lines make visible what Mbembe calls the "hidden logics of slum survival": how people navigate limited space, scarce resources, and constant threat through mental mapping.

This spatial dialectic also represents what Mbembe terms the "aesthetics of precarity." Each chess piece's movement—the pawn's slow advance, the knight's lateral jump, the queen's powerful diagonal—mirrors the different survival tactics available in Katwe. This creates a cognitively mapped hierarchy of mobility: some moves (and some lives) have more options than others. This structural insight makes conventional uplift narratives nearly impossible, as any individual success must contend with the geometric constraints of the board (and the social constraints of the slum).

The Film as Mbembian Case Study

Queen of Katwe ultimately functions as what Mbembe would call a "study in postcolonial improvisation." The slum represents not just poverty but the necropolitical condition of urban Africa: a world where life is organized around the management of scarcity rather than the distribution of abundance. The chess matches symbolize what Mbembe calls "cognitive battles within material constraint."

Yet the film's strength—its rating of 9—lies in what Mbembe might call its "refusal of sentimentalism." The inspiration isn't sugar-coated; the floods remain, the poverty persists, the system continues. Where conventional sports films present triumph as system-transcending, this film shows triumph as system-navigating. The chessboard's operation is both literal and metaphorical: real chess, real slum, real constraints, real improvisation. Real necropolitics, as Mbembe shows, requires this dual vision: seeing both the material brutality and the cognitive resistance.

The film also brilliantly explores what Mbembe identifies as the "psychic dimensions of improvisation": how subjects develop mental strategies to survive physical deprivation. While it shows the physical effects of poverty, it equally portrays the psychological development of tactical thinking that Mbembe describes—how people in zones of abandonment become masters of constraint navigation.

Conclusion: Chess as Postcolonial Epistemology

Queen of Katwe serves as a profound cinematic exploration of Mbembian thought. It visualizes key concepts—necropolitical zones, brutalist improvisation, tactical cognition, informal infrastructures—with emotional precision. Yet it remains grounded in what Mbembe would call "the materiality of the postcolony": the mud, the water, the scarcity, the constant negotiation with death.

Real necropolitical resistance, as Mbembe demonstrates, is less about revolutionary overthrow than about what he terms "improvisational survival"—finding cracks in systems, creating alternative networks, developing cognitive maps of constraint. The film's strength is making this improvisation visible; its relative weakness (the missing point to 10/10) is its occasional concession to Disney-fied inspirational beats that soften the Mbembian analysis.

Ultimately, Queen of Katwe earns its 9/10 rating: it successfully applies Mbembian concepts to biographical narrative, creating a powerful allegory of postcolonial improvisation. Like Phiona's pawn that becomes a queen, it shows how tactical intelligence can transform constraint into possibility—but only within the board's sixty-four squares, only within the slum's flooded boundaries. It gives us the chess game but doesn't let us forget the water rising around the board—or, in Mbembian terms, it shows us cognitive resistance while reminding us that the floodwaters of necropolitical abandonment continue to rise.
