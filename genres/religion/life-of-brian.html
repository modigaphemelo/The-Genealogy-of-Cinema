Life of Brian

Hitchens' Messiah Complex as Herd Morality Parody
Year 1979
Director Terry Jones
Analysis via Christopher Hitchens
Genre Comedy / Satire

Terry Jones' Life of Brian presents itself as a comedic retelling of a first-century Judean's accidental messianic career. Through Christopher Hitchens' lens of anti-theistic critique, it reveals itself as a devastating parody of what Hitchens called "religion poisons everything"—the human tendency to transform ordinary figures into divine authorities and simple wisdom into dogmatic systems. The film's central joke—Brian Cohen's mistaken identity as the Messiah—becomes not just a comedic device but the structural manifestation of Hitchens' argument about the "herd mentality" of religious devotion.

The Architecture of Absurd Devotion

Hitchens, in his polemic God Is Not Great, argues that religious faith represents "the wish to be a slave"—a surrender of critical thinking to authority figures and ancient texts. The followers of Brian in the film embody this precise tendency: they interpret his every action, however mundane, as divine revelation. When Brian loses a sandal, they fight over the "holy footwear"; when he tells them to "go away," they transform it into "Go away... and multiply." This isn't mere absurdity—it's what Hitchens would call "the literal interpretation syndrome," where believers actively seek meaning in meaningless events.

"He's not the Messiah, he's a very naughty boy!"

This line, delivered by Brian's mother Mandy Cohen (Terry Jones in drag), captures the film's central Hitchensian insight. The problem is not Brian—he's an ordinary man caught in extraordinary circumstances—but the collective delusion of his followers. Those around him exercise what Hitchens terms "credulity as virtue"—they actively suppress their critical faculties in favor of belief. This is not just comedy but something more profound: anti-theistic satire in its purest form, where religious devotion is revealed as the abdication of reason.

The Three Anti-Religious Satirical Modes

The film operates through three distinct satirical strategies, each representing what Hitchens would call different "manifestations of religious absurdity":

  1. Institutional Absurdity: The People's Front of Judea, the Judean People's Front, and other splinter groups parody what Hitchens calls "religious sectarianism as petty politics." Their endless debates about whether Romans "go home" or "fuck off" mirror theological disputes over doctrine. As Hitchens noted, religious schisms often have less to do with theology than with "personality and power."
  2. Scriptural Literalism: The followers' interpretation of Brian's mundane utterances as divine commandments satirizes what Hitchens termed "biblical exegesis as creative writing." Their transformation of "Blessed are the cheesemakers" into profound theology demonstrates how believers "read meaning into, not out of" sacred texts.
  3. Messianic Projection: The crowd's insistence that Brian is the Messiah despite his protests embodies what Hitchens called "the wish fulfillment of divinity." As he argued, humans "invent gods to worship" rather than discover them—Brian becomes a messiah because people need one, not because he qualifies.

The Crucifixion as Collective Singalong

The film's controversial ending—Brian and others crucified while singing "Always Look on the Bright Side of Life"—functions as what Hitchens would term "cosmic irony as therapeutic nihilism." In a system organized around divine salvation through suffering, the song represents not optimism but the ultimate humanist response to absurdity. It's both the most tragic (facing execution) and the most liberating (rejecting theological meaning-making). This mirrors Hitchens' analysis of how religious frameworks create "metaphysical prisons" from which the only escape is laughter.

When Brian joins the singing on the cross, he participates in what Hitchens calls "the atheist's last laugh"—using humor to deflate religious pretension in the face of mortality. Their collective chorus becomes a secular alternative to prayer, rejecting both divine intervention and despair in favor of human resilience. From the petty squabbles of revolutionary groups to the absurd devotion of followers, each element represents a different "theological folly"—a different way humans complicate simple existence with unnecessary supernatural baggage.

The Success of Anti-Theistic Satire

The film's enduring power—earning its 10/10 rating—lies in what Hitchens might call its "satirical precision." Unlike crude anti-religious polemic, Life of Brian uses what Hitchens praised as "humor as the solvent of faith": it doesn't attack believers but the belief system itself. The followers aren't evil—they're ordinary people caught in the machinery of religious thinking. This reflects Hitchens' distinction between criticizing religion and criticizing religious people: "The enemy is not the believer, but the belief."

Religious Bureaucracy as Comedic Target

The film's bureaucratic elements—from Roman occupation to revolutionary committees—are essential to its anti-theistic critique. Hitchens emphasized that organized religion operates through "clerical hierarchies as power structures" that cannot be reduced to individual faith. Pontius Pilate's speech impediment and the bureaucratic nightmare of crucifixion paperwork make visible what Hitchens calls the "mundane reality behind sacred spectacle": how religious institutions are human organizations with all their flaws, not divine establishments.

This institutional focus also represents what Hitchens termed "the professionalization of piety." The religious authorities—both Roman and Judean—aren't evil masterminds but careerists and functionaries. This creates a cascading absurdity of religious administration: each character is simultaneously part of the system and its victim, enforcing rules they don't understand on people who don't want them. This institutional structure makes escape nearly impossible, as any rebellion must contend not with divine will but with human bureaucracy.

The Film as Hitchensian Primer

Life of Brian ultimately functions as what Hitchens would call "satirical pedagogy" for anti-theistic thought. The film represents not just religious belief but the human psychology of belief systems: our tendency to follow leaders, seek patterns in randomness, and prefer comforting myths to uncomfortable truths. The gourd and sandal debate symbolizes what Hitchens calls "theological disputes as displaced anxiety."

The film's strength—its perfect rating—lies in what Hitchens might call its "comic generosity." The satire is sharp but not cruel, intelligent but not elitist. Where Hitchens' writing could be polemical, the film presents a compassionate critique. The characters aren't idiots—they're humans doing what humans do: looking for meaning, community, and purpose. Real religious devotion, as Hitchens shows, often comes from these universal human needs, not from evil intent.

The film also perfectly captures what Hitchens identified as the "performative aspects of piety": how religious practice involves social signaling, group conformity, and identity performance. While it shows the physical comedy of crucifixion, it brilliantly portrays the social theater of belief that Hitchens described—how people adopt religious identities as social roles rather than personal convictions.

Conclusion: The Enduring Power of Satirical Atheism

Life of Brian serves as the perfect cinematic expression of Hitchensian thought. It visualizes key concepts—religious herd mentality, institutional absurdity, scriptural literalism, messianic projection—with comedic brilliance. Yet it remains grounded in what Hitchens would call "humanist compassion": the understanding that believers are victims of belief systems, not their architects.

Real anti-theism, as Hitchens demonstrated, requires both intellectual rigor and emotional intelligence—understanding why people believe as well as why they shouldn't. The film's strength is making this dual perspective accessible; its weakness is that some viewers mistake its targets. As Hitchens noted, satire often "reveals the believer to themselves" in ways direct argument cannot.

Ultimately, Life of Brian earns its 10/10 rating: it successfully applies Hitchensian critique to comedic form, creating the perfect anti-theistic satire. Like the gourd that becomes holy because people fight over it, it shows how religious meaning is created through social conflict rather than divine revelation. It gives us the cross but doesn't let us take it seriously—or, in Hitchensian terms, it shows us religious theater while reminding us there are no gods in the audience, only humans in the seats.
