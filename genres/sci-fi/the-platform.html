The Platform

Mbembe's Necropolitical Verticality: Hunger as Sovereign Power
Year 2019
Director Galder Gaztelu-Urrutia
Analysis via Achille Mbembe
Genre Sci-Fi / Horror

Galder Gaztelu-Urrutia's The Platform presents itself as a dystopian horror about a vertical prison where food descends from top to bottom. Through Achille Mbembe's lens of necropolitics, it reveals itself as a brutal allegory for how modern sovereignty operates not through biopower (the power to foster life) but through necropower - the power to dictate who may live and who must die. The film's central mechanism - the descending platform of food - becomes not just a plot device but the architectural manifestation of what Mbembe calls the "work of death" as the ultimate expression of political authority.

The Architecture of Death

Mbembe, in his seminal work Necropolitics, argues that contemporary power expresses itself through "the right to kill" and the organization of "death worlds" - spaces where entire populations are reduced to a state of living death. The Vertical Self-Management Center in The Platform is precisely such a death world. Each level is not merely a floor but a zone of mortality, with the upper levels constituting what Mbembe would call "zones of life" (where subjects experience relative comfort) and the lower levels "zones of death" (where subjects exist in a state of perpetual starvation). This vertical hierarchy is the spatialization of necropower: sovereignty literally flows downward, just as the platform descends.

"It's not the food that's scarce. It's the goodwill."

This line, delivered by Trimagasi (Zorion Eguileor), the cynical veteran of the pit, captures the film's central necropolitical insight. The problem is not material scarcity - the platform overflows with food on the upper levels - but the distribution of death. Those above exercise what Mbembe terms the "droit de glaive" (the right of the sword) over those below. They decide, through their consumption habits, who will live and who will starve. This is not capitalism but something more primal: necropolitical sovereignty in its purest form, where power is measured not in wealth but in the capacity to allocate mortality.

The Three Necropolitical Subjects

The film presents three archetypal responses to necropolitical conditions, each representing what Mbembe would call different "forms of life" under the regime of death:

  1. Goreng (Iván Massagué): The enlightened neoliberal who believes in "spontaneous solidarity." He enters with a copy of Don Quixote and faith in human rationality. His failure represents the bankruptcy of Enlightenment reason in the face of necropolitical reality. As Mbembe notes, necropolitics operates through "irrational excess" not rational calculation.
  2. Trimagasi (Zorion Eguileor): The cynical survivor who has internalized the necropolitical logic. He represents what Mbembe calls the "living dead" - a subject who continues biological existence but has accepted their social death. His cannibalism is not barbarism but the logical endpoint of a system where human bodies become "raw material" for survival.
  3. Baharat (Emilio Buale Coka): The revolutionary who attempts to implement "vertical solidarity." His panna cotta plan represents what Mbembe might call "necropolitical resistance" - an attempt to weaponize the very logic of the system against itself. Yet his failure reveals the trap: in fighting the necropolitical system, one often reproduces its logic.

The Child as Messianic Exception

The film's most controversial element - the child as the "message" - functions as what Mbembe would term a "necropolitical exception." In a system organized around death, the child represents not innocence but the exception that proves the rule. She is both the most vulnerable (requiring protection) and the most powerful (the supposed solution). This mirrors Mbembe's analysis of how necropolitical regimes often create "zones of exception" where normal rules are suspended. The child exists outside the platform's economy of hunger, yet her presence transforms everything.

When Goreng and Baharat descend with the child on the platform, they attempt to create what Mbembe calls a "counter-necropolitics" - using the system's own infrastructure to challenge its logic. Their journey downward becomes a necropolitical pilgrimage, witnessing each level's particular adaptation to the economy of death. From the religious fanaticism of the lower levels to the cannibalistic pragmatism of the middle, each floor represents a different "necropolitical subjectivity" - a different way of organizing life around the inevitability of death.

The Failure of Revolutionary Messianism

The film's ending - where the child ascends as the "message" but the system continues unchanged - is a profoundly Mbembian conclusion. It suggests that messianic solutions fail against necropolitical systems. The child's ascension doesn't dismantle the vertical prison; it merely creates a new myth to sustain it. This reflects Mbembe's skepticism about "revolutionary eschatology": the belief that a single act or figure can overturn systems of death. Necropolitical power, Mbembe argues, is too diffuse, too embedded in infrastructure and everyday practices, to be overthrown by symbolic gestures.

Verticality as Necropolitical Metaphor

The film's vertical architecture is not accidental but essential to its necropolitical critique. Mbembe emphasizes that necropower operates through "vertical relations of domination" that cannot be reduced to horizontal class struggle. The platform's descent makes visible what Mbembe calls the "topography of cruelty": how power flows from top to bottom, how privilege depends on others' suffering, and how vertical distance enables moral detachment.

This verticality also represents what Mbembe terms the "fractalization of sovereignty." Each level has its own micro-sovereign (the cell's dominant prisoner) who exercises limited necropower over their immediate inferiors. This creates a cascading hierarchy of death-dealing: each subject is simultaneously victim and perpetrator, oppressed from above and oppressing those below. This fractal structure makes resistance nearly impossible, as any rebellion must contend not with a single sovereign but with an entire vertical network of micro-sovereignties.

The Platform as Necropolitical Allegory

The Platform ultimately functions as what Mbembe would call a "necropolitical allegory" for contemporary global inequality. The vertical prison represents not a specific political system but the necropolitical condition of late modernity: a world organized around the differential allocation of life and death chances. The food on the platform symbolizes what Mbembe calls "vital resources" in a world of artificial scarcity.

Yet the film's weakness - its rating of 7 - lies in what Mbembe might call its "allegorical overdetermination." The metaphor is perhaps too obvious, too heavy-handed. Where Mbembe's analysis is subtle, tracing necropolitical power through capillary networks and discursive formations, the film presents a blunt mechanical allegory. The platform's operation is almost too literal, too transparent. Real necropolitics, as Mbembe shows, operates through more insidious mechanisms: bureaucratic procedures, legal exceptions, medical categories, and security protocols that disguise the work of death behind the facade of administration.

The film also fails to explore what Mbembe identifies as the "psychic dimensions of necropower": how subjects internalize their own expendability, how the expectation of death shapes consciousness. While it shows the physical effects of starvation, it doesn't adequately portray the psychological normalization of mortality that Mbembe describes - how people come to accept death as an ordinary part of life under necropolitical regimes.

A Defense of Bluntness

However, this very bluntness might be the film's strength for popular audiences. Mbembe's work, while essential, remains academically dense and inaccessible to many. The Platform translates complex necropolitical theory into visceral, embodied experience. Its mechanical allegory functions as what Brazilian educator Paulo Freire would call "critical consciousness-raising" - making abstract systems of oppression visible and tangible. For viewers who will never read Mbembe, the film's brutal clarity may serve as an essential first encounter with necropolitical logic. The question becomes whether philosophical subtlety should be sacrificed for political accessibility - a dilemma the film cannot resolve but at least productively raises.

Conclusion: Allegory and Its Limits

The Platform serves as an effective introduction to Mbembian thought, visualizing key concepts - necropower, vertical sovereignty, death worlds, fractal domination - with brutal clarity. Yet it remains partially trapped within the "allegorical impulse": the desire to reduce complex historical processes to simple mechanical metaphors. Real necropolitics, as Mbembe demonstrates, is messier, more contradictory, and more insidious than the film's vertical prison. It operates through what he terms "the becoming-black of the world" - not literal skin color but a generalized condition of expendability that can affect any population marked as surplus.

Ultimately, The Platform earns its 7/10 rating: it successfully applies Mbembian concepts to cinematic form, creating a powerful allegory of necropolitical power. But like the panna cotta that never reaches the bottom, it offers a message that may not fully survive translation into the complex realities of actual necropolitical systems. Its greatest achievement - making necropolitics visible - is also its limitation, suggesting that the work of death may finally resist representation, especially in forms as tidy as allegory. Whether this limitation is failure or necessary compromise depends on whether we believe cinema should illuminate or complicate - and whether, in Mbembian terms, illumination without complication can ever truly challenge the necropolitical order.
